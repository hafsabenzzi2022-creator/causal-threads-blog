Trusted Biotech and Health Security for Allied Resilience

Biodefense, AI governance, and clinical evidence are debated in separate rooms. They are one strategic system — and trusted evidence is the infrastructure that holds it together.

H
Dr. Hafsa Benzzi
Dermatologist · PhD Candidate, Mount Sinai · Causal Inference Researcher

Most of what I write here lives in a clinical register — statins, biologics, the architecture of a directed acyclic graph. This piece is deliberately different. It belongs in a security venue as much as a medical one, because the questions it raises are not really about medicine. They are about resilience.

When an institution like the Atlantic Council convenes on biological threats, artificial intelligence, and transatlantic resilience, it is asking a question that clinical journals rarely frame out loud: what does an allied society need to be able to trust when a biological event arrives? Not which drug works — but which evidence can be acted on, fast, across borders, under pressure, without breaking public confidence. That is not a bench-science problem. It is an infrastructure problem. And it is the reason a physician-scientist who works on causal inference and real-world evidence has something specific to contribute to a policy conversation.

The Problem: Three Conversations That Should Be One

Walk through the agenda of almost any serious policy institution and you will find three workstreams running in parallel, rarely touching.

The first is biodefense — preparedness against natural, accidental, and deliberate biological threats. It lives in security centers and national-security commissions, and it speaks the language of detection, deterrence, and response.

The second is AI governance — how societies should build, audit, and trust algorithmic systems. It lives in technology-policy programs, and it speaks the language of transparency, bias, and accountability.

The third is evidence infrastructure — the unglamorous machinery of how we know what we know in medicine: data systems, study design, regulatory standards, the methods that separate a real effect from a confounded one. It lives, mostly, inside academic medicine and regulatory agencies, and almost never reaches a policy stage at all.

These three are treated as separate problems handled by separate communities. In a crisis, they collapse into a single one. A novel pathogen is, simultaneously, a detection problem (biodefense), an interpretation problem (the models we use to read the signal are AI systems), and an evidence problem (we have to decide what counts as good-enough proof to authorize a countermeasure while people are dying). The fault lines between the three communities become the fault lines of the response.

The Argument: Evidence Infrastructure Is Strategic Infrastructure

Here is the claim I want to put on the table: trusted evidence systems are strategic infrastructure for biological resilience — in the same category as ports, grids, and secure communications.

We already accept this logic everywhere else. We do not wait for a blackout to think about the resilience of the grid. We treat the capacity to generate and move reliable information as a thing worth defending in advance. Yet when it comes to biological resilience, we still treat the evidence system as a back-office function — something that happens after the science, rather than the thing that determines whether the science can be trusted and used at all.

This matters because the next biological event will not be adjudicated by a single randomized trial completed at leisure. It will be adjudicated by real-world evidence generated under time pressure, across heterogeneous health systems, interpreted by models, and consumed by a public deciding whether to comply. Whether that evidence is trustworthy — not just available — is a security variable.

"Resilience is usually imagined as the ability to absorb a shock. But for biological threats, half of resilience is epistemic: the ability to know what is happening, and to be believed when you say it."

The methodological discipline that makes observational evidence trustworthy — causal inference, target-trial thinking, explicit reasoning about confounding and bias — is not a niche academic concern. It is the quality-control layer of a strategic asset. When that layer is weak, two things follow: bad decisions get made on confounded data, and good decisions lose public trust because the reasoning behind them cannot be explained. Both are resilience failures.

Five Places This Becomes Concrete

Abstraction is easy; the value is in where these conversations actually intersect. Five examples — each one a place where biodefense, AI, and evidence are already colliding.

1 · Federated biodefense needs federated evidence

Modern biodefense is moving toward federated, networked detection — many institutions, many data streams, no single owner. But a federated sensing system is only as good as the federated evidence system behind it. If sites cannot pool analyses without pooling raw patient data, and cannot agree on what a credible signal looks like, the network detects noise it cannot act on. The causal-methods community has spent a decade on exactly this: how to estimate effects across distributed cohorts without a single shared dataset. That work belongs in the biodefense conversation.

2 · Explainable clinical AI and the right to a reason

When an algorithm flags a patient, a population, or a countermeasure, "the model said so" is not an acceptable basis for a policy decision under public scrutiny. Explainability is often framed as a technical nicety. In a health-security context it is a trust requirement: officials must be able to state why a system recommended what it did, in causal terms a non-specialist can follow. Explainable AI and causal inference are natural allies here — both are, at root, about replacing correlation with reasons.

3 · Pharmacogenomics and preparedness

Countermeasures do not act uniformly across a population. Genetic variation changes who responds, who is harmed, and at what dose — a reality I've written about for statins, where a single variant can rewrite how a standard drug behaves. A preparedness strategy that assumes an average patient is planning for a population that does not exist. Building genomic stratification into countermeasure evidence — before the crisis — is a resilience investment, not a luxury.

4 · EU–US data and governance alignment

Allied resilience is only as strong as the seams between allies. Europe is building the European Health Data Space; the United States runs large national research programs under different governance assumptions. If trusted evidence is strategic infrastructure, then transatlantic alignment on how that evidence is generated, validated, and shared is a security objective in its own right. This is squarely transatlantic-policy terrain, not a clinical footnote.

5 · Morocco, the Atlantic Initiative, and the Global South bridge

Biological threats do not respect the borders of the alliance, and resilience built only among wealthy states is brittle. Morocco has already articulated an Atlantic-facing vision of exactly this kind. The Atlantic Initiative — launched by King Mohammed VI in November 2023 to connect the landlocked Sahel states to the Atlantic through Moroccan ports and infrastructure — treats Atlantic connectivity as a shared platform for development and regional integration across Atlantic-facing Africa. Health-security resilience is the natural next layer of that same connectivity: the corridors built to move trade can also carry surveillance capacity, countermeasure logistics, and trusted evidence. Having practiced and trained across Morocco, France, and the United States, I see the bridge clearly — methods and standards built in well-resourced systems have to be made portable to settings with different data, different infrastructure, and different trust dynamics. A credible Global South partner that already frames itself around the Atlantic is worth more to allied resilience than another redundant high-income one.

The Causal Thread

The reason this sits on a causal-inference blog is not a stretch. The discipline of asking what actually causes what — of refusing to let correlation stand in for a reason — is the same discipline a society needs when it has to act on imperfect evidence under pressure and still be believed. Biodefense asks whether we can detect a threat. AI governance asks whether we can trust the systems that read it. Evidence infrastructure asks whether what we conclude is real. Those are not three questions. They are one question asked three times.

Trusted biotech is not only about better molecules. It is about an evidence system allied societies can lean on when it matters most. Treat that system as strategic infrastructure, and resilience stops being a slogan and starts being something you can actually build.

The thread connecting a genome, a model, and a national-security decision is a causal one. It is past time we traced it across the rooms where it actually runs.
